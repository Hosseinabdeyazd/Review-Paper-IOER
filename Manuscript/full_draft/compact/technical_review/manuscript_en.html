From GeoAI to environmental impact assessment: A systematic review of uncertainty continuity and digital twin architecture for the circular built environment

Introduction · Related work · Methods · Results · Discussion

Complete-text working draft, without abstract. Numerical coding was reproduced; all-search eligibility and complete semantic recoding are not closed. Empirical frequencies and conceptual architecture diagrams are explicitly distinguished.

1. Introduction

The circular built environment depends on understanding how existing buildings become future material resources and how alternative recovery pathways affect environmental outcomes. Material production is a substantial source of greenhouse gas emissions, while material efficiency and changes in construction materials can alter future resource demand and carbon balances (Hertwich, 2021; Pauliuk et al., 2021; Churkina et al., 2020). Circularity therefore requires more than increasing recycling rates: it requires connecting knowledge of existing stocks with technically feasible recovery and environmentally meaningful substitution. Reviews of circular construction identify diverse practices, conceptual frameworks and implementation barriers across building life cycles (Joensuu et al., 2020; Çimen, 2021; Mhatre et al., 2021; Akhimien et al., 2021). These perspectives motivate a connected assessment of the evidence supporting circular decisions, including the uncertainty that accompanies each transformation from observation to environmental consequence.

Integrated assessments provide the starting point for examining this challenge. Mastrucci and colleagues connect GIS-based building-stock characterisation and a spatiotemporal database with life-cycle assessment of end-of-life scenarios to support urban waste-management decisions (Mastrucci et al., 2017). Ajayebi and colleagues integrate building geometries and construction histories with product-stock estimates and embodied-carbon mapping for prospective urban mining (Ajayebi et al., 2020). Their temporal characterisation and reuse-oriented inventory do not, however, establish a complete forecast of recoverable outflows. Pei and colleagues review integrated building-material-stock and LCA workflows encompassing data collection, stock modelling and result evaluation (Pei et al., 2024). These studies establish connected pathways while differing in temporal treatment, recovery modelling and environmental scope.

The Forest to Building Digital Framework additionally connects geospatial, production and building information with actor-based LCA and decision support in a wood-construction supply chain (Morganti et al., 2025). Its boundary differs from urban mining, but it illustrates connections across technical and organisational subsystems. These integrated approaches motivate examination of complete workflows before decomposing their transformations into analytical interfaces. The resulting stages must be justified through comparison, rather than imposed as a universal five-stage sequence. The research boundary extends from spatial information to material stocks and flows, recovery, environmental assessment and decision support.

GeoAI expands observational capabilities through the interpretation of heterogeneous spatial data (Wang et al., 2024; Mai et al., 2025). Mapping built-structure mass and combining geospatial and image analysis for demolition-waste modelling illustrate links between observation and material accounting (Frantz et al., 2023; Bogoviku and Waldmann, 2021). These developments complement established GIS methods; they do not imply that earlier integrated frameworks implemented GeoAI. Downstream estimates depend on inferred attributes, their resolution and validity, and assumptions translating them into material quantities.

Within-archetype variability and correlations between building size and material intensity complicate this translation (Dai et al., 2025; Miatto et al., 2023). Integrated BIM--GIS approaches organise stock information across representations (Elshaboury et al., 2025). Yet more detailed inventories do not automatically establish how uncertainty in inferred attributes affects estimated mass. Interfaces therefore concern both numerical propagation and consistency of the entities, classifications and assumptions linking models.

Subsequent transformations introduce temporal and scenario dependencies. Lifespan assumptions, renovation and future development affect when stocks become flows and their composition (Miatto et al., 2017; Göswein et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2021; Arehart et al., 2022). Dynamic LCA further highlights changing assessment conditions through time (Slavkovic and Stephan, 2025). The analytical question is whether such transformations preserve upstream uncertainty and dependencies or substitute independently specified scenarios.

Recovery assessment evaluates whether released materials or components can enter subsequent use pathways. Material passports can support end-of-life information management, while probabilistic reusability assessment explicitly addresses technical eligibility (Honic et al., 2021; Rakhshan et al., 2021). Accordingly, an estimated outflow is not equivalent to a reusable supply. Condition, contamination, separation and recovery assumptions may change both the eligible quantity and its assigned destination. Environmental assessment then translates these pathways into impacts. Assessment methods for component reuse, life-cycle databases and design-stage assessment practices influence how burdens and benefits are represented (De Wolf et al., 2020; De Wolf et al., 2023; Roberts et al., 2020). Uncertainty must also be distinguished from variability when interpreting environmental results (Michiels and Geeraerd, 2020). The recovery--impact interface therefore connects uncertain recovery outcomes with assumptions about substitution, system boundaries and environmental characterisation.

Computational connection alone does not establish continuity of uncertainty. Exchanging an output need not transfer its uncertainty, dependencies or conditions of validity. Distinguishing uncertainty from variability is already important within LCA (Michiels and Geeraerd, 2020); across models, these distinctions must survive changes in entities, spatial aggregation, temporal horizons and methods. Here, uncertainty continuity denotes the traceable retention or explicit revision of uncertainty information and relevant dependencies across linked transformations. Existing integration and dynamic-assessment reviews provide foundations (Pei et al., 2024; Slavkovic and Stephan, 2025). The question pursued here is how far interface-level continuity is demonstrated, where it breaks down and what structural requirements follow, without presuming that uncertainty has been universally neglected.

Digital twins offer a potential architectural basis for investigating these connections. Semantic construction twins and broader characterisations of digital twins emphasise relationships between physical entities, digital representations and updating processes (Boje et al., 2020; Jones et al., 2020). Construction reviews document enabling technologies and applications, while also revealing variation in implementation and maturity (Opoku et al., 2021; Tuhaise et al., 2023). Wider reviews and built-environment definition studies likewise caution against treating the digital-twin label as evidence of a uniform capability (Sharma et al., 2022; Abdelrahman et al., 2025). Building-twin frameworks provide further ways to organise acquisition, integration, modelling and analysis (Zhang et al., 2025). For circular material assessment, the relevant issue is therefore which demonstrated capabilities can preserve meaningful relationships between observations, material states, future flows and environmental calculations.

Uncertainty-aware digital-twin research provides complementary methodological resources. Reviews of uncertainty quantification and software risk, and probabilistic graphical formulations of predictive twins, show how uncertainty can be represented alongside linked models and updated information (Lin et al., 2021; Kapteyn et al., 2021). Their application to circular building flows, however, requires examining domain compatibility. This review investigates persistent entity identification, information provenance, probabilistic representations, dependency management, updating and decision communication as candidate architectural functions. Spatial scale, temporal validity and stakeholder information requirements are treated as cross-cutting dimensions. Neither continuous sensing nor increased model detail is assumed to reduce uncertainty automatically; the proposed architecture must instead make supported transformations and unresolved limitations inspectable.

The objective is to derive an evidence-traceable digital-twin architecture for uncertainty continuity from spatial and GeoAI-supported information to environmental impact assessment and decision support. Integrated workflows are first compared to define analytical interfaces from their documented transformations. The review then connects evidence on uncertainty at these interfaces, domain-specific digital-twin applications and transferable uncertainty-related mechanisms. Four questions guide the synthesis:

RQ1. How do existing integrated frameworks connect spatial information, material stocks and flows, recovery, environmental assessment and decision support, and which interfaces emerge from their comparison?

RQ2. What uncertainty information and dependencies are transferred, transformed, updated or lost across these interfaces, and how is this demonstrated?

RQ3. Which demonstrated digital-twin capabilities address the identified interface requirements, and under what spatial, temporal and information-governance conditions?

RQ4. How can these findings be synthesised into an architecture that distinguishes directly supported components, cross-study synthesis and proposals addressing documented gaps?

Section 2 reviews the conceptual foundations and related work. Section 3 specifies the connected search, selection, interface extraction and synthesis methods. Section 4 presents the evidence analysis and architectural synthesis. Section 5 discusses interpretation, limitations and validation priorities. The contribution is a justified architectural synthesis; operational effectiveness remains a question for subsequent validation.

2. Related work and conceptual foundations

Circular material assessment connects information produced for different purposes: observations describe buildings, stock models estimate material content, dynamic models predict releases, and recovery and environmental assessments evaluate subsequent use. Integrated studies establish this connection. Mastrucci et al. (2017) link geospatial stock characterization with life-cycle assessment of end-of-life scenarios; Ajayebi et al. (2020) connect building information with spatial and temporal product-stock estimates; and Pei et al. (2024) review stock–LCA integration. Their boundaries differ, but together they motivate comparison of the transformations linking observations to circular decisions.

This comparison organizes the review around five activities: observing and inferring building characteristics (S1), estimating material stocks (S2), modelling stock change and outflows (S3), evaluating recovery and subsequent use (S4), and assessing environmental consequences and supporting decisions (S5). These labels identify comparable activities, not a universal architecture implemented by every study. Workflows may combine, omit or revisit them. GeoAI extends the observational activity through spatial inference, while image-based demolition-waste modelling illustrates its connection to material accounting (Wang et al., 2024; Bogoviku and Waldmann, 2021).

The interfaces introduce substantive assumptions. S1→S2 associates building attributes with material composition or intensity. Schiller et al. (2019) show that transferring composition indicators between German and Japanese residential buildings requires harmonization and attention to building type and context; coefficients are not universally interchangeable. Building-size–intensity relationships and lifespan assumptions also influence stock and release estimates (Miatto et al., 2023; Miatto et al., 2017). At S2→S3, a stock becomes a dated flow. Shared attributes and parameters can therefore create dependencies between upstream and downstream estimates that should not be discarded during model coupling.

At S3→S4, an estimated outflow must be distinguished from an available secondary resource. Material passports address information needed for end-of-life management, while probabilistic reusability assessment evaluates technical suitability (Honic et al., 2021; Rakhshan et al., 2021). Condition, separation and quality influence the recoverable quantity and its destination. S4→S5 then connects those outcomes to environmental accounting, including the treatment of subsequent use and substitution (De Wolf et al., 2020). These boundaries remain analytically relevant even when one model performs several activities.

Uncertainty and variability have different meanings in this chain (Michiels and Geeraerd, 2020). Incomplete knowledge about an attribute, variation among buildings and alternative future scenarios cannot automatically be interpreted as equivalent probability distributions. We use uncertainty continuity to mean the traceable retention or explicit revision of uncertainty information, dependencies and validity conditions across transformations. Continuity does not require a distribution to remain unchanged: propagation, new observations or aggregation may legitimately alter it. The question is whether that alteration is documented and usable downstream. Missing reporting alone does not establish that uncertainty was lost in the implementation.

Digital-twin research supplies a complementary perspective on connected representations and updating. Systematic reviews characterize DT concepts and construction applications, while semantic construction twins emphasize information integration (Jones et al., 2020; Boje et al., 2020; Opoku et al., 2021; Tuhaise et al., 2023). These functions require separate assessment: a geometric model need not maintain a data connection, and an updated representation need not propagate uncertainty. Abdelrahman et al. (2025) distinguish real-time operational twins from longer-term decision-support applications. For material assessment, a survey, renovation or recovery inspection may be the relevant update trigger; a continuous sensor stream is not itself evidence of a valid connected assessment.

Uncertainty-aware DT research offers more specific mechanisms. Kapteyn et al. (2021) demonstrate probabilistic modelling, calibration and updating in an aircraft-related application, while Lin et al. (2021) review uncertainty quantification and software risks. Their mechanisms motivate examination of state representation, dependencies and revision procedures, but do not demonstrate continuity throughout the circular-material chain. Transfer requires matching entities, quantities, time horizons and validation conditions. Likewise, a scalable software architecture does not establish that material assumptions remain valid across buildings, districts and regions.

The review connects these literatures through their interfaces: material-flow research identifies what is transformed; uncertainty research identifies what information must be retained or revised; DT research identifies capabilities for linking evidence and calculations. Spatial support, temporal validity and stakeholder information requirements qualify these connections. Building on stock–LCA, uncertainty and DT reviews, the analysis distinguishes directly documented connections, cross-study synthesis and proposals addressing gaps. Its contribution is an evidence-traceable architectural synthesis, rather than a newly implemented or validated end-to-end twin.

3. Materials and methods

3.1. Review design: a shared interface as the unit of synthesis

The review connects evidence on material-assessment workflows, uncertainty and digital-twin capabilities through a shared analytical unit: a documented interface between activities. Search families provide complementary routes to this evidence, rather than separate reviews with unrelated conclusions. The design addresses four linked questions: which transformations connect S1–S5; what happens to uncertainty at those transformations; which DT capabilities are relevant under the reported conditions; and how the findings support an evidence-traceable architecture. PRISMA 2020 guides reporting of study selection and PRISMA-S guides documentation of searches (Page et al., 2021; Rethlefsen et al., 2021). These reporting guidelines do not replace the extraction and synthesis procedures specified below.

For publication p and interface i→j, the extraction record is

E(p,i,j) = {entity, input, transformation, output, uncertainty-in, uncertainty-out, dependencies, DT-function, context, evidence}

. Entity identifies the building, component or material represented; context includes spatial support, temporal validity and information-user requirements; evidence contains the publication identifier and page, section, equation or figure locator. DT-function is recorded as not applicable or not reported where appropriate. Thus a non-DT material-flow study and a DT mechanism study can be compared through an explicit information requirement without claiming that they implement the same system. Figure 1 shows this connected workflow; Supplementary Figure S1 specifies the analytical joins.

3.2. Information sources and search families

The database searches cover Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection and IEEE Xplore. Base, also denoted S, refers to stage-specific building/material-flow searches. U denotes uncertainty and DT denotes digital twin. The domain families are Base, Base+U, Base+DT and Base+DT+U. General DT and DT+U searches provide definition and transferable-mechanism evidence, with their non-domain origin retained. Supplementary searches for integrated workflows are not restricted by publication year or publisher. Exact filters are documented per search execution rather than assumed identical across historical runs.

Scopus searches use TITLE-ABS-KEY; Web of Science searches use Topic, which also includes Keywords Plus. IEEE field selections and split-query variants are retained in the execution records. The logical search structure is Q(i,k) = B(i,k) AND S(i) AND optional(U) AND optional(DT), where k identifies the query version. The version index matters because some historical DT searches use a broader domain block than earlier Base searches. The logical diagram is therefore not evidence that every historical query forms a strict subset of one identical Base query.

Supplementary Table S1 lists the concepts used to organize retrieval. The complete archived strings, including exclusions and database adaptations, remain in Supplement S1 and the recovered S1–S3 query files. Missing historical execution details are flagged in the audit rather than reconstructed as executed searches. New explanatory examples in this section are not presented as additional database searches.

The search-block table, counting equations and reproducibility checks are provided in the supplement. Continuity distributions count assessed paper–boundary contexts, with one status per context. Stage–function edges count distinct publications carrying the exact paired code; memberships overlap and are not summed as unique papers.

For clarity, the four domain combinations are B AND S(i); B AND S(i) AND U; B AND S(i) AND DT; and B AND S(i) AND DT AND U. General DT+U omits the building-stage restriction. Broad U terms improve retrieval but do not establish uncertainty analysis: sensitivity, calibration or robustness must be interpreted from the methods. Membership is recorded at publication level after deduplication. Actual overlaps are calculated from identifiers, not inferred from query labels or summed database counts (Rethlefsen et al., 2021).

3.3. Eligibility, deduplication and selection

Each publication is screened against its intended evidential role. Domain studies must describe a relevant activity or transformation in building-material assessment. U evidence must identify an affected quantity, assumption or decision and explain its treatment; a generic uncertainty statement is insufficient for a mechanism claim. DT evidence must describe representations, data connections, updating or analytical functions. Architecture papers can provide conceptual evidence without being relabelled operational implementations. External-domain mechanisms and secondary reviews are retained separately as complementary evidence. A paper need not address all five stages or demonstrate successful uncertainty continuity to be relevant.

Original database exports are preserved. DOI values are normalized; records without DOI are checked using native identifiers and bibliographic information. Similar titles, conflicting DOIs and related conference/journal versions require adjudication. Duplicate publications are distinguished from multiple reports of one study. Multiple search memberships are retained without multiplying the unique-publication denominator.

Selection proceeds through identification, deduplication, title/abstract screening, full-text retrieval, full-text eligibility and inclusion. Full text is checked for identity, completeness and methodological detail. Abstract-only material and incomplete previews do not support full-text mechanism extraction. Reports that cannot be obtained or adequately assessed do not enter the final synthesis. Their records remain in the selection audit: non-retrieval is distinguished from exclusion after assessment, and available but unread reports remain pending rather than being assigned a scientific exclusion reason. No target number of included publications is imposed.

Full-text exclusions have a primary reason, with secondary notes where needed: outside the material-flow domain; no relevant DT function for a DT-specific synthesis; no substantive U treatment for a U-specific synthesis; or insufficient methodological evidence for the intended analytical role. A report can be ineligible for the strict triple intersection while remaining relevant to a complementary stream. Reasons and evidence locators are preserved. PRISMA reporting follows these distinctions (Page et al., 2021).

3.4. Interface extraction and uncertainty coding

Stage assignment is based on the operation actually performed. An edge S(i)→S(j) requires a documented use of an upstream output in a downstream activity; mentioning two stages does not establish an edge. Non-adjacent links and feedback are retained. Table 2 defines the common questions applied across the four adjacent interfaces. These are extraction prompts, not findings that every study implements the listed transformations.

S2→S3Inventory to time-dependent releaseInventory uncertainty, lifetimes, survival and scenario dependenciesState/time representation, linked dynamic models and versioning

The extraction distinguishes uncertainty source, nature, representation and operation. Parameter, model and scenario uncertainty are recorded separately from spatial/temporal variability where the study permits this distinction (Michiels and Geeraerd, 2020). Representations include distributions, samples, intervals, scenarios and qualitative quality statements. Dependencies include reported covariance, correlation, shared parameters and explicit independence assumptions. Missing dependence information is not coded as independence.

The interface state uses non-exclusive operation codes: transferred, transformed, updated, newly introduced, or demonstrably discarded. A separate reporting status identifies insufficient description. Reduced uncertainty requires a stated comparison and metric; improved prediction accuracy alone does not establish reduction. Source statements are distinguished from reviewer interpretation. Each mechanism claim requires a locator and a short evidence note. Scale, temporal resolution, update trigger, validity period and stakeholder information roles are extracted as conditions of the interface, not as disconnected descriptive categories.

3.5. Linking interface requirements to DT capabilities

DT coding records the authors' definition, physical referent, digital representation, data connection, update direction/frequency, analytical service and demonstrated maturity (Jones et al., 2020; Boje et al., 2020; Abdelrahman et al., 2025). Real-time operation and bidirectional control are not imposed as universal requirements. Nor is a BIM/GIS model automatically treated as an operational DT. The distinction between probabilistic modelling and demonstrated asset–twin updating is informed by uncertainty-aware DT research (Kapteyn et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2021).

For each interface, an evidence chain links: observed limitation → information requirement → reported DT capability → compatibility conditions → supported architectural connection. Compatibility is checked against the entity, variable meaning, unit, spatial support, temporal horizon and dependency representation. For example, a general-domain Bayesian updating mechanism is not assigned to S3–S4 merely because both involve uncertainty; the proposed connection must explain what recovery-related state would be updated, by which observation and under which assumptions. If that connection is inferred across studies, it is explicitly coded as synthesis rather than a demonstrated domain implementation.

3.6. Relational analysis and architectural synthesis

The same interface records generate three connected matrices: interface × uncertainty operation; interface × DT capability; and DT capability × uncertainty operation. Every populated cell resolves to supporting publication and evidence identifiers. A fourth join table links all three dimensions and records whether the connection occurs within one study or is synthesized across studies. No complete S1–S5 chain is inferred simply by concatenating unrelated papers. Supplementary Figure S1 shows the shared record that prevents the three matrices from becoming independent analyses.

For a given eligible corpus C, the cell count is n(i,u,d) = the number of distinct publications in C supporting interface i, uncertainty operation u and DT capability d together. Direct within-publication counts and cross-study synthesis are reported separately; inferred links do not inflate empirical frequencies. Coverage of a coded property is its distinct supporting-publication count divided by the number of eligible, assessed publications for that analysis. The denominator and unreported cases accompany each figure. Multilabel coding means marginal totals can exceed the unique-publication total. This treatment follows the need to distinguish study counts from category participation, illustrated in Falsafi et al. (2025) (Falsafi et al., 2025).

Descriptive outputs include evidence heatmaps, overlap plots and interface networks. A line represents a coded connection, not causality; thickness represents a specified count, not effectiveness. Temporal counts summed across databases are labelled database occurrences unless record-level deduplication supports unique-publication counts. Statistical tests are not assumed valid for overlapping categories, and visual differences are not labelled significant without an appropriate test design. Heterogeneous outcomes are not pooled in an effectiveness meta-analysis.

Architectural elements are classified as directly supported, synthesized from compatible studies, or proposed in response to a documented gap. The architecture thus links each module and connection to its evidential basis and to the interface requirement it addresses. A reported limitation, missing reporting and an absence in the retrieved corpus remain distinct. The resulting structure is a review-based conceptual synthesis; operational effectiveness and cross-scale generalization require subsequent validation.

3.6.1. Exact positive-code configurations (D03)

For the non-review DT–U subset, each publication is represented by z(p)=(z1,...,z7), where zk=1 for a reported R-coded mechanism and 0 otherwise. The seven dimensions are probabilistic representation, Bayesian state/parameter inference, explicit dependency transport, residual/shift diagnostics, online adaptation, interval-coverage assessment and uncertainty-linked alerts/actions. The original C and NR states are preserved separately: zero in this projection does not establish absence. Publications assigned to the review/reference-architecture family are omitted from this configuration analysis.

For each observed binary pattern v, its exact-intersection frequency is h(v)=sum_p 1[z(p)=v]. Each publication contributes to exactly one pattern, and sum_v h(v)=N. Marginal mechanism frequency is m(k)=sum_v v(k)h(v). Columns are sorted by decreasing h, then number of positive codes, with a deterministic binary-pattern tie-break. This is an UpSet-style exact-set display, not clustering, a maturity ranking or an inferred causal sequence. Identical positive-code patterns do not establish identical implementations; implementations may differ in context and in their C/NR states.

3.6.2. Source–method associations within the same transition context (D04)

Source and method labels are joined by publication and assessed context. Let A(q) denote contexts recording uncertainty source q and A(m) those recording method m. The number recording both is:

n(q,m)=|A(q) intersection A(m)|

The denominator counts all contexts recording source q:

N(q)=|A(q)|

The within-source co-occurrence percentage is:

P(m|q)=100 n(q,m)/N(q)

Empty source rows have undefined percentages. Rows and columns follow assessment frequency, with codebook order resolving ties.

Because labels are multilabel, percentages within a row need not sum to 100%. The join establishes co-occurrence within one assessed context, not that the method was specifically applied to that source. For example, a context containing two sources and two methods yields four co-occurrence cells; a source-specific treatment claim requires an additional explicit evidence relation. Source labels are not automatically mapped to epistemic or aleatory uncertainty. Prediction-error diagnostics and scenario comparison are not automatically probabilistic propagation.

3.6.3. Joining the four outputs into transition-based architectural evidence

D01 establishes the recorded continuity state of a boundary; D04 identifies accompanying sources and methods; D02 locates reported domain functions; D03 provides general-DT mechanism configurations. The architectural join uses the boundary record as its anchor and stores: boundary_id, material variable and unit, uncertainty source/representation, continuity state, method, DT function, update trigger, spatial/temporal validity, evidence locator and support class. Within-publication links are distinguished from compatible cross-study synthesis and proposed additions. General DT configurations are not relabelled S1–S5 implementations.

For each boundary, the results will report the assessed publication count, context count, distribution of continuity states, documented variable transformations, candidate DT functions and the evidential status of each proposed connection. A transition diagram can therefore show what is preserved, transformed, newly introduced or insufficiently reported without interpreting a stage–function edge as uncertainty transport. Missing B34 coverage remains visible. A conceptual missing link is not filled by reallocating O5 assessments to B45 or by treating a review as an independent experiment.

The architectural synthesis additionally maps each configuration family to its proposed uncertainty responsibility, material-interface application and required output. The extraction separates a source-supported concept from our allocation of that concept to S1–S5. Unresolved descriptors retain that status. A mapped capability is therefore a design requirement unless a source demonstrates the corresponding operation; candidate connections in the configuration diagram are not counted as empirical observations.

3.7. Evidence appraisal, traceability and reporting status

Appraisal considers data provenance, explicit assumptions, dependency treatment, validation, scenario conditions and reproducibility. Synthetic demonstrations, empirical tests and conceptual proposals are distinguished. A universal numerical quality score is not imposed on heterogeneous designs. The decision ledger retains source, role, eligibility reason and evidence locator, while code and count inputs accompany figures. The review does not claim an independent two-human-reviewer process where none is documented.

4. Results

4.1. Evidence sets and the meaning of the reported percentages

The linked analyses use 20 included S1–S5+U publications for source, method and outcome frequencies. These same publications contribute 23 publication–context assessments of continuity. The DT stage–function network uses nine publications with S1–S5 function codes, and the general DT+U configuration analysis uses ten non-review publications. Those denominators answer different questions and are not added as independent review totals.

The source, method and outcome dataset combines the 17 previously coded publications with three newly coded full texts, joined by DOI to the 20 included reports in the selected-set eligibility ledger. Sources include explicitly acknowledged as well as quantified uncertainties; the new decision table distinguishes these states. Each paper contributes at most once to each category. This update reconciles membership and arithmetic, while retaining the provenance of earlier interpretive codes.

The percentages are frequencies of recorded evidence. For example, 70.0% means that fourteen of twenty publications carry a particular source code. It does not mean that this source contributes 70.0% of output variance. Similarly, the proportion of papers reporting Monte Carlo is not a measure of model accuracy, uncertainty magnitude or the effectiveness of uncertainty reduction.

4.2. Sources, representations and downstream outcomes

Material intensity/composition occurs in 14/20 (70.0%), followed by scenario/future context in 9/20 (45.0%). Temporal/event and LCA inventory/factor sources each occur in 8/20 (40.0%); model-form uncertainty occurs in 7/20 (35.0%). Recovery/technical feasibility is recorded in 2/20 (10.0%). These frequencies describe documented sources, including acknowledged limitations; they are not the proportion of studies that successfully propagate each source. For example, Caruso et al. (2026) explicitly acknowledge but do not model component-quantity uncertainty.

The pattern places material representation and future assumptions at the centre of the observed uncertainty discussion. It also shows why a single label such as “data uncertainty” is insufficient: uncertain building attributes, material coefficients, lifetimes and environmental factors enter different transformations. Source categories do not automatically determine whether uncertainty is epistemic, aleatory or a mixture; that distinction depends on the model, observational support and decision context (Michiels and Geeraerd, 2020; Vilaysouk et al., 2021; Miatto et al., 2017; Cao et al., 2017).

4.3. Where continuity is documented across the four boundaries

The current boundary coding separates evidence of an uncertain input from evidence of its downstream treatment. At S1→S2, nine contexts are assessed. Four (44.4%) do not demonstrate downstream transfer; two (22.2%) provide sensitivity/representation comparisons; one each (11.1%) provides review/discussion, prepares inputs for propagation, or documents partial transfer. None is coded as quantified output uncertainty for selected inputs in this boundary-specific classification. Material-intensity distributions and prediction-error measures can therefore improve the input description without establishing a complete uncertain stock estimate (Vilaysouk et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2023).

At S2→S3, six contexts are assessed: three (50.0%) document partial transfer, two (33.3%) quantify outputs for selected uncertain inputs, and one (16.7%) does not demonstrate transfer. The stronger evidence here is consistent with the explicit use of lifetime distributions, probabilistic parameters and dynamic material-flow models (Miatto et al., 2017; Cao et al., 2017; Heeren and Hellweg, 2018; Liu et al., 2026). However, an uncertain lifetime model can coexist with deterministic upstream quantities. The evidence does not justify interpreting the entire chain as probabilistic simply because the stock-dynamics calculation uses sampling.

No S3→S4 context is assessed in this particular harmonised coding set. A percentage is therefore undefined, rather than zero percent successful propagation. The sole S4→S5 context is coded as partial transfer: 1/1, or 100% of one observation. This is a case description, not a stable estimate of methodological prevalence (Almeida et al., 2026). The computational model performs inference and propagation. GeoAI may provide geometry, attribute probabilities or prediction errors; inventory models translate these into material quantities; lifetime models produce release trajectories; recovery models apply condition and yield; environmental models evaluate consequences. Physics-based, data-driven and hybrid models can each support this operation when their uncertain inputs, parameters, discrepancy and dependencies are specified. The internal architecture makes that operation traceable. Modules expose input/output contracts; semantic identifiers connect observations, models and material entities; provenance records the origin and version of each assumption. Coupling and temporal organization determine whether estimates remain current. New survey evidence, renovation or recovery inspection can trigger a revision; provenance identifies downstream outputs requiring recalculation. Continuous updating is not necessary for every circular decision, and two-way exchange does not establish autonomous control. Agency determines who initiates checks, requests additional evidence, selects a model or approves an action. Agent-based simulation models interacting entities; multi-agent orchestration distributes service tasks among software actors. Agentic AI denotes goal-directed tool orchestration here, but remains unsupported as a specific configuration in this source set. Actors may request evidence or invoke checked estimators; they do not replace inference or validation. Human authority and escalation remain explicit (Kritzinger et al., 2018; Shao and Helu, 2020; Agrawal et al., 2023).

5. Discussion

5.1. Continuity is a property of interfaces, not the presence of UQ software

The central implication is that uncertainty-aware components do not automatically form an uncertainty-continuous workflow. GeoAI can produce an error score, a stock model can use variable material coefficients, and a lifecycle model can run Monte Carlo, yet these activities may refer to different uncertain inputs. The review therefore shifts the question from whether a method appears in a paper to whether the information needed by the next transformation survives the interface. Integrated stock–flow and impact workflows motivate this question, while the transition analysis specifies how it can be examined (Bogoviku and Waldmann, 2021; Pei et al., 2024; Mastrucci et al., 2017; Ajayebi et al., 2020).

Three operations must be distinguished. Propagation computes consequences of specified uncertain inputs. Updating changes an uncertainty representation in light of observations or revised assumptions. Validation evaluates whether predictions and their uncertainty are adequate for the intended use. Running one operation does not establish the other two. In particular, a small prediction error does not establish calibrated predictive intervals, and a narrower posterior does not establish that missing mechanisms have been resolved. A digital twin is useful here because it can organise repeated observation, model revision and evidence evaluation, provided these operations are implemented explicitly (Kapteyn et al., 2021; Shao and Helu, 2020).

5.2. What must cross each material-flow boundary?

At S1→S2, the transformation links inferred building attributes to material quantities. A schematic stock calculation is

M = Σ_b A_b I_b

, where A denotes a relevant geometric measure and I a compatible material-intensity coefficient. The uncertainty of the product depends on both factors and their dependence. Transferring only the predicted building class or mean coefficient loses information needed by the inventory model. A useful DT interface would retain class probabilities or alternative attribute states, material-coefficient uncertainty, consistent units and the aggregation rule. It should not turn a classification confidence score into a calibrated probability without supporting evaluation.

At S2→S3, an inventory is transformed into dated outflows using age, lifetime, renovation and event assumptions. A schematic mapping

O(t) = Σ_c M_c f_c(t | age, scenario)

emphasises that the output is a flow in a time interval rather than a stock at a date. The uncertainty representation must therefore carry material quantities together with the event-time model. A new building survey can update stock information, while a renovation event may revise remaining life and outflow timing. Revisions must preserve their relationship to the previous state rather than silently overwrite the forecast (Miatto et al., 2017; Cao et al., 2017; Heeren and Hellweg, 2018; Liu et al., 2026).

At S3→S4, potential outflow becomes technically recoverable and route-specific material.

R = O × η × q

is a deliberately schematic expression: η represents yield and q a quality/acceptance factor, and their meanings must be defined for the selected reuse or recycling route. Damage, contamination and selective-disassembly evidence may change these factors and their dependence. The current absence of a coded context at this boundary makes a condition-observation and recovery-evaluation interface a justified research priority, but not a validated solution. It would be inappropriate to assign universal recovery distributions to fill that gap (Honic et al., 2021; Rakhshan et al., 2021).

At S4→S5, recovered materials acquire environmental consequences under substitution, allocation and system-boundary assumptions. A schematic impact calculation sums route-specific material quantities multiplied by environmental factors and subtracts eligible avoided burdens. The same recovered mass can lead to different results when substituted products, quality equivalence or future energy systems differ. A traceable interface must retain those conditions along with material quality and quantity. Circularity alone does not establish an environmental benefit, and a reported impact interval cannot be interpreted as end-to-end uncertainty unless upstream recovery uncertainty is included (Almeida et al., 2026; De Wolf et al., 2020; Morganti et al., 2025).

These expressions illustrate interface requirements; the review has not fitted or executed a combined material-flow model. Their role is to make the architectural requirements precise enough for subsequent empirical testing.

5.3. How the twin could detect, track and explain uncertainty

The proposed architecture uses a common evidence contract across the four boundaries and allocates different checks to the functions that can perform them. The observation service checks completeness, calibration, coverage and timing. The inference service estimates states or parameters and records posterior or other supported uncertainty. The propagation service passes compatible samples, distributions or scenario identifiers through a stated transformation. The diagnostic service compares predictions with independent observations. The decision service expresses the consequences of the remaining uncertainty for an authorised choice. Identity, provenance and versions connect these services, while stakeholder roles determine who may revise assumptions, approve evidence or act on outputs (Human et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2023; Kosse et al., 2025).

A useful diagnostic is a residual

r = y_observed − y_predicted

. A persistent or unexpectedly large residual can indicate a problem, but may originate in measurement noise, stale input, parameter error, model discrepancy or changed conditions. Diagnosis therefore requires assumptions about both observation and prediction. Under an appropriate predictive model, a standardised residual may divide r by the square root of predictive plus observational variance, including dependence terms where relevant. Such a diagnostic must not be presented as a universal detector or given an arbitrary threshold.

Tracking requires more than storing successive uncertainty widths. A change from a stock interval in tonnes to an impact interval in kilograms CO2-equivalent is a transformation in quantity and unit. Directly comparing the numerical variances would be meaningless. Comparisons of amplification or reduction require a common quantity, compatible scale and context, or a stated normalisation. Likewise, aggregation can reduce relative variability when errors offset while preserving a shared bias. These distinctions explain why the outcome sketches are a conceptual vocabulary, not numerical results for each stage.

The uncertainty representation should remain plural. Sampling or Bayesian inference is useful when probabilistic assumptions are supported; scenario comparisons remain appropriate when future alternatives do not have defensible probabilities. A qualitative limitation should be carried as a limitation rather than assigned an invented distribution. This allows a stakeholder to see both the calculated uncertainty and what the calculation has left outside its scope (Michiels and Geeraerd, 2020; Kapteyn et al., 2021).

5.4. Why computation, integration and agency have different roles

Table 2 separates estimation from continuity. A computational model estimates uncertainty; semantic and coupling services retain its meaning across transformations; diagnostics test predictions against observations; agency schedules these activities and assigns accountability. A multi-agent system coordinates distinct services, whereas an agent-based simulation represents interacting entities within a model. Neither term guarantees uncertainty quantification. Agentic AI is a proposed orchestration option here, not a capability validated by this review.

Similarly, spatial scalability requires explicit aggregation and shared-error treatment. Updating a building-level estimate must identify which district inventory, flow forecast and impact result depend on it. This dependency tracking is the proposed connection between the general DT architecture and circular material-flow analysis; its end-to-end performance remains to be tested.

5.5. Limits and research priorities

The quantitative distributions describe the 20-publication selected set, not population prevalence. Seventeen publications retain their archived coding and three have newly documented source-level decisions. The 23-context continuity analysis uses a separate evidence-status taxonomy on the same publication set. The small general-DT sample remains cross-domain. These distributions cannot rank method effectiveness or establish complete propagation through every S1–S5 boundary.

No pooled percentage reduction in uncertainty is reported. Such a synthesis would require extracting the quantity of interest, units, probabilistic assumptions, sample or scenario basis, uncertainty metric, upstream/downstream comparison and validation conditions from each eligible primary study. RMSE, coefficient of variation, confidence intervals, prediction intervals and scenario ranges cannot be pooled merely because all describe some form of spread or error. The existing extraction does not provide a harmonised basis for that operation.

The next empirical test should focus on one fully documented boundary before attempting the entire chain. It should compare deterministic transfer with a dependency-preserving alternative, retain identical input evidence, evaluate held-out observations where available, and test how changes affect the decision rather than interval width alone. Particular priority belongs to S3→S4 and to the connection between recovery quality and environmental substitution. Completing pending eligibility and page-specific evidence reconciliation remains necessary before this working manuscript can be submitted as a closed systematic review.

6. Conclusion

This review connects material-flow analysis, uncertainty assessment and digital twin concepts to examine what survives the interfaces between S1 and S5. Its central finding is that uncertainty analysis within several stages does not establish continuity between them. The selected 20-publication uncertainty set contributes 23 interface/context assessments, with stronger documented connections at S2→S3 and no assessed S3→S4 context in this extraction. These observations locate gaps in the selected evidence; they do not demonstrate absence across the wider literature.

The architectural synthesis assigns complementary responsibilities to computational models, semantic interfaces, temporal updating, diagnostics and decision roles. Models estimate and propagate uncertainty. Identity, provenance and dependence information make its passage traceable. Validation tests whether predictions remain adequate for their intended use, while governance determines who can revise assumptions or act. The proposed interface requirements connect these functions to GeoAI-derived attributes, inventories, outflows, recovery and environmental consequences without claiming an implemented or universally validated twin.

The practical research priority is to test a documented boundary where uncertain inputs, transformations and downstream decisions can be compared under consistent conditions, especially release-to-recovery and recovery-to-impact links. The contribution is an evidence-linked organisation of requirements and gaps, rather than a new uncertainty algorithm or a measured reduction in uncertainty. Its present conclusions remain bounded by the selected, assessed corpus and the incomplete eligibility of broader retrieval sets.
