# From GeoAI to environmental impact assessment: A systematic review of uncertainty continuity and digital twin architecture for the circular built environment

## 1. Introduction

The circular built environment depends on understanding how existing buildings become future material resources and how alternative recovery pathways affect environmental outcomes. Material production is a substantial source of greenhouse gas emissions, while material efficiency and changes in construction materials can alter future resource demand and carbon balances ([Hertwich, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-021-00690-8); [Pauliuk et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-25300-4); [Churkina et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-019-0462-4)). Circularity therefore requires more than increasing recycling rates: it requires connecting knowledge of existing stocks with technically feasible recovery and environmentally meaningful substitution. Reviews of circular construction identify diverse practices, conceptual frameworks and implementation barriers across building life cycles ([Joensuu et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2020.124215); [Çimen, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2021.127180); [Mhatre et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2020.101995); [Akhimien et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2020.102041)). These perspectives motivate a connected assessment of the evidence supporting circular decisions, including the uncertainty that accompanies each transformation from observation to environmental consequence.

This review organises that assessment into five analytical stages, S1–S5. S1 concerns observation and inference of building attributes. Geospatial artificial intelligence (GeoAI) expands the ability to interpret spatial data and infer characteristics of the built environment ([Wang et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jag.2024.103734); [Mai et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jag.2025.104368)). Mapping the mass of built structures and combining geospatial and image analysis for demolition-waste modelling demonstrate the relevance of spatial observations to material accounting ([Frantz et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43755-5); [Bogoviku and Waldmann, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2020.111879)). However, observations, inferred attributes and modelling assumptions have different evidential status. Their spatial resolution, temporal validity and prediction errors establish conditions for subsequent calculations. S1 must therefore be examined as the origin of both useful information and dependencies that downstream models may preserve or discard.

S2 translates building attributes into material composition and quantities. This translation commonly connects geometry, building classifications and material-intensity information. Evidence of variability within housing archetypes and correlation between building size and material intensity challenges the treatment of these inputs as uniform or independent ([Dai et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2025.108392); [Miatto et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2023.107093)). Integrated BIM–GIS approaches offer mechanisms for organising building-stock information across representations ([Elshaboury et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2025.113391)). Yet a more detailed inventory does not automatically establish how uncertainty in inferred attributes contributes to uncertainty in estimated mass. The S1→S2 interface consequently concerns both numerical propagation and the consistency of the entities, classifications and assumptions linking observations to inventories.

S3 converts stocks into time-dependent additions, transformations and outflows. Building-lifespan assumptions affect demolition-waste estimates, while renovation, material choices and future development scenarios change the timing and composition of material flows ([Miatto et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2017.01.015); [Göswein et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2021.107773); [Tang et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2020.105298); [Arehart et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2022.106583)). Dynamic life-cycle assessment further highlights the importance of representing buildings and their consequences through time ([Slavkovic and Stephan, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rser.2024.115262)). At S2→S3, uncertainty thus acquires a temporal dimension: an uncertain quantity becomes a distribution of possible release dates and flow magnitudes. A central analytical question is whether this transformation retains upstream variability and dependencies or replaces them with independently specified scenarios.

S4 evaluates whether released materials or components can enter recovery pathways. Material passports can support end-of-life information management, while probabilistic reusability assessment explicitly addresses technical eligibility ([Honic et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2021.128702); [Rakhshan et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2021.102791)). Accordingly, an estimated outflow is not equivalent to a reusable supply. Condition, contamination, separation and recovery assumptions may change both the eligible quantity and its assigned destination. S5 then translates these pathways into environmental impacts. Assessment methods for component reuse, life-cycle databases and design-stage assessment practices influence how burdens and benefits are represented ([De Wolf et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scs.2020.102322); [De Wolf et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2022.106642); [Roberts et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2020.107274)). Uncertainty must also be distinguished from variability when interpreting environmental results ([Michiels and Geeraerd, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2020.104841)). The S4→S5 interface therefore connects uncertain recovery outcomes with assumptions about substitution, system boundaries and environmental characterisation.

Together, these stages expose a question that cannot be resolved by counting uncertainty analyses within individual models. Integration of building-material stock analysis and urban-scale life-cycle assessment is already an identifiable research field ([Pei et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2024.111741)); nevertheless, computational connection alone does not demonstrate continuity of uncertainty. Here, uncertainty continuity denotes the traceable retention or explicit revision of uncertainty information and relevant dependencies across linked transformations. Its assessment requires identifying what crosses each boundary, how its representation changes and what is omitted. A reported range at two successive stages, for example, does not establish that the second range incorporates the first. This distinction provides the review's analytical focus rather than assuming that an end-to-end gap has already been demonstrated.

Digital twins offer a potential architectural basis for investigating these connections. Semantic construction twins and broader characterisations of digital twins emphasise relationships between physical entities, digital representations and updating processes ([Boje et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autcon.2020.103179); [Jones et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cirpj.2020.02.002)). Construction reviews document enabling technologies and applications, while also revealing variation in implementation and maturity ([Opoku et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2021.102726); [Tuhaise et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autcon.2023.104931)). Wider reviews and built-environment definition studies likewise caution against treating the digital-twin label as evidence of a uniform capability ([Sharma et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jii.2022.100383); [Abdelrahman et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2025.112748)). Building-twin frameworks provide further ways to organise acquisition, integration, modelling and analysis ([Zhang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2025.113117)). For circular material assessment, the relevant issue is therefore which demonstrated capabilities can preserve meaningful relationships between observations, material states, future flows and environmental calculations.

Uncertainty-aware digital-twin research provides complementary methodological resources. Reviews of uncertainty quantification and software risk, and probabilistic graphical formulations of predictive twins, show how uncertainty can be represented alongside linked models and updated information ([Lin et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anucene.2021.108362); [Kapteyn et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-021-00069-0)). Their application to circular building flows, however, requires examining domain compatibility. This review investigates persistent entity identification, information provenance, probabilistic representations, dependency management, updating and decision communication as candidate architectural functions. Spatial scale, temporal validity and stakeholder information requirements are treated as cross-cutting dimensions. Neither continuous sensing nor increased model detail is assumed to reduce uncertainty automatically; the proposed architecture must instead make supported transformations and unresolved limitations inspectable.

The objective is to synthesise an evidence-traceable digital-twin architecture for following uncertainty from GeoAI-supported observations to environmental impact assessment in the circular built environment. Three complementary evidence streams are connected: uncertainty across S1–S5, digital twins applied to these stages, and transferable uncertainty-related capabilities in digital-twin research. Four questions guide the review:

RQ1. What uncertain quantities, assumptions and dependencies are represented within S1–S5 and at their interfaces?

RQ2. How are these uncertainties transferred, transformed, updated or lost between consecutive stages, and what evidence supports those characterisations?

RQ3. Which demonstrated digital-twin capabilities address the identified interface requirements, and under what spatial, temporal and information-governance conditions?

RQ4. How can these findings be synthesised into an architecture that distinguishes directly supported components, cross-study synthesis and proposals addressing documented gaps?

The contribution is an architectural synthesis whose components and connections remain traceable to their evidential basis, rather than a claim that a complete operational twin has been validated. Section 2 presents the search, screening, extraction and synthesis methods. Section 3 analyses the three evidence streams and their interface-level relationships. Section 4 develops the architecture and identifies the status of its supporting evidence. Section 5 discusses implications, limitations and validation priorities, and Section 6 presents the conclusions.
