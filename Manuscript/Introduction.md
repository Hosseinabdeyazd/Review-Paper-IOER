# From GeoAI to environmental impact assessment: A systematic review of uncertainty continuity and digital twin architecture for the circular built environment

## 1. Introduction

The circular built environment depends on understanding how existing buildings become future material resources and how alternative recovery pathways affect environmental outcomes. Material production is a substantial source of greenhouse gas emissions, while material efficiency and changes in construction materials can alter future resource demand and carbon balances ([Hertwich, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-021-00690-8); [Pauliuk et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-25300-4); [Churkina et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-019-0462-4)). Circularity therefore requires more than increasing recycling rates: it requires connecting knowledge of existing stocks with technically feasible recovery and environmentally meaningful substitution. Reviews of circular construction identify diverse practices, conceptual frameworks and implementation barriers across building life cycles ([Joensuu et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2020.124215); [Çimen, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2021.127180); [Mhatre et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2020.101995); [Akhimien et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2020.102041)). These perspectives motivate a connected assessment of the evidence supporting circular decisions, including the uncertainty that accompanies each transformation from observation to environmental consequence.

Integrated assessments provide the starting point for examining this challenge. Mastrucci and colleagues connect GIS-based building-stock characterisation and a spatiotemporal database with life-cycle assessment of end-of-life scenarios to support urban waste-management decisions ([Mastrucci et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2016.07.003)). Ajayebi and colleagues integrate building geometries and construction histories with product-stock estimates and embodied-carbon mapping for prospective urban mining ([Ajayebi et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2020.105026)). Their temporal characterisation and reuse-oriented inventory do not, however, establish a complete forecast of recoverable outflows. Pei and colleagues review integrated building-material-stock and LCA workflows encompassing data collection, stock modelling and result evaluation ([Pei et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2024.111741)). These studies establish connected pathways while differing in temporal treatment, recovery modelling and environmental scope.

The Forest to Building Digital Framework additionally connects geospatial, production and building information with actor-based LCA and decision support in a wood-construction supply chain ([Morganti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2025.146074)). Its boundary differs from urban mining, but it illustrates connections across technical and organisational subsystems. These integrated approaches motivate examination of complete workflows before decomposing their transformations into analytical interfaces. The resulting stages must be justified through comparison, rather than imposed as a universal five-stage sequence. The research boundary extends from spatial information to material stocks and flows, recovery, environmental assessment and decision support.

GeoAI expands observational capabilities through the interpretation of heterogeneous spatial data ([Wang et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jag.2024.103734); [Mai et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jag.2025.104368)). Mapping built-structure mass and combining geospatial and image analysis for demolition-waste modelling illustrate links between observation and material accounting ([Frantz et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-43755-5); [Bogoviku and Waldmann, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2020.111879)). These developments complement established GIS methods; they do not imply that earlier integrated frameworks implemented GeoAI. Downstream estimates depend on inferred attributes, their resolution and validity, and assumptions translating them into material quantities.

Within-archetype variability and correlations between building size and material intensity complicate this translation ([Dai et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2025.108392); [Miatto et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2023.107093)). Integrated BIM–GIS approaches organise stock information across representations ([Elshaboury et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2025.113391)). Yet more detailed inventories do not automatically establish how uncertainty in inferred attributes affects estimated mass. Interfaces therefore concern both numerical propagation and consistency of the entities, classifications and assumptions linking models.

Subsequent transformations introduce temporal and scenario dependencies. Lifespan assumptions, renovation and future development affect when stocks become flows and their composition ([Miatto et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2017.01.015); [Göswein et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2021.107773); [Tang et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2020.105298); [Arehart et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2022.106583)). Dynamic LCA further highlights changing assessment conditions through time ([Slavkovic and Stephan, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rser.2024.115262)). The analytical question is whether such transformations preserve upstream uncertainty and dependencies or substitute independently specified scenarios.

Recovery assessment evaluates whether released materials or components can enter subsequent use pathways. Material passports can support end-of-life information management, while probabilistic reusability assessment explicitly addresses technical eligibility ([Honic et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclepro.2021.128702); [Rakhshan et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2021.102791)). Accordingly, an estimated outflow is not equivalent to a reusable supply. Condition, contamination, separation and recovery assumptions may change both the eligible quantity and its assigned destination. Environmental assessment then translates these pathways into impacts. Assessment methods for component reuse, life-cycle databases and design-stage assessment practices influence how burdens and benefits are represented ([De Wolf et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scs.2020.102322); [De Wolf et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resconrec.2022.106642); [Roberts et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2020.107274)). Uncertainty must also be distinguished from variability when interpreting environmental results ([Michiels and Geeraerd, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2020.104841)). The recovery–impact interface therefore connects uncertain recovery outcomes with assumptions about substitution, system boundaries and environmental characterisation.

Computational connection alone does not establish continuity of uncertainty. Exchanging an output need not transfer its uncertainty, dependencies or conditions of validity. Distinguishing uncertainty from variability is already important within LCA ([Michiels and Geeraerd, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2020.104841)); across models, these distinctions must survive changes in entities, spatial aggregation, temporal horizons and methods. Here, uncertainty continuity denotes the traceable retention or explicit revision of uncertainty information and relevant dependencies across linked transformations. Existing integration and dynamic-assessment reviews provide foundations ([Pei et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2024.111741); [Slavkovic and Stephan, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rser.2024.115262)). The question pursued here is how far interface-level continuity is demonstrated, where it breaks down and what structural requirements follow, without presuming that uncertainty has been universally neglected.

Digital twins offer a potential architectural basis for investigating these connections. Semantic construction twins and broader characterisations of digital twins emphasise relationships between physical entities, digital representations and updating processes ([Boje et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autcon.2020.103179); [Jones et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cirpj.2020.02.002)). Construction reviews document enabling technologies and applications, while also revealing variation in implementation and maturity ([Opoku et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2021.102726); [Tuhaise et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autcon.2023.104931)). Wider reviews and built-environment definition studies likewise caution against treating the digital-twin label as evidence of a uniform capability ([Sharma et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jii.2022.100383); [Abdelrahman et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.buildenv.2025.112748)). Building-twin frameworks provide further ways to organise acquisition, integration, modelling and analysis ([Zhang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jobe.2025.113117)). For circular material assessment, the relevant issue is therefore which demonstrated capabilities can preserve meaningful relationships between observations, material states, future flows and environmental calculations.

Uncertainty-aware digital-twin research provides complementary methodological resources. Reviews of uncertainty quantification and software risk, and probabilistic graphical formulations of predictive twins, show how uncertainty can be represented alongside linked models and updated information ([Lin et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anucene.2021.108362); [Kapteyn et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43588-021-00069-0)). Their application to circular building flows, however, requires examining domain compatibility. This review investigates persistent entity identification, information provenance, probabilistic representations, dependency management, updating and decision communication as candidate architectural functions. Spatial scale, temporal validity and stakeholder information requirements are treated as cross-cutting dimensions. Neither continuous sensing nor increased model detail is assumed to reduce uncertainty automatically; the proposed architecture must instead make supported transformations and unresolved limitations inspectable.

The objective is to derive an evidence-traceable digital-twin architecture for uncertainty continuity from spatial and GeoAI-supported information to environmental impact assessment and decision support. Integrated workflows are first compared to define analytical interfaces from their documented transformations. The review then connects evidence on uncertainty at these interfaces, domain-specific digital-twin applications and transferable uncertainty-related mechanisms. Four questions guide the synthesis:

RQ1. How do existing integrated frameworks connect spatial information, material stocks and flows, recovery, environmental assessment and decision support, and which interfaces emerge from their comparison?

RQ2. What uncertainty information and dependencies are transferred, transformed, updated or lost across these interfaces, and how is this demonstrated?

RQ3. Which demonstrated digital-twin capabilities address the identified interface requirements, and under what spatial, temporal and information-governance conditions?

RQ4. How can these findings be synthesised into an architecture that distinguishes directly supported components, cross-study synthesis and proposals addressing documented gaps?

Section 2 presents the search, screening, extraction and synthesis methods. Section 3 compares integrated workflows and analyses their interfaces and supporting evidence. Section 4 develops the architecture with explicit evidence traceability. Sections 5 and 6 discuss implications, limitations and validation priorities and present the conclusions. The contribution is a justified architectural synthesis; operational effectiveness remains a question for subsequent validation.
