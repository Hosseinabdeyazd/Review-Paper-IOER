# 2. Related work and conceptual foundations

Understanding circular material opportunities in the built environment requires connecting information that is normally produced for different purposes. Spatial observations describe buildings, stock models estimate their material content, dynamic models estimate future releases, and recovery and environmental assessments evaluate what those releases might mean for subsequent use. Integrated studies provide a basis for examining this connected workflow. Mastrucci et al. (2017) link geospatial characterization of building material stocks with life cycle assessment of end-of-life scenarios, while Ajayebi et al. (2020) address the spatial and temporal quantification of structural products for a prospective circular economy. Pei et al. (2024) review the techniques and tools connecting building material stock analysis and urban-scale life cycle assessment. These contributions establish integration as a substantive research problem, although their system boundaries and treatment of individual transformations differ [I36,I37,I35].

Comparison of these workflows motivates five analytical activities: observing and inferring building characteristics (S1), estimating material stocks (S2), modelling stock change and material outflows (S3), evaluating recovery and subsequent use (S4), and assessing environmental consequences and supporting decisions (S5). The labels are the organizing vocabulary of this review, rather than a five-stage architecture attributed to every cited study. They make it possible to compare equivalent transformations across different modelling traditions. Some studies combine activities, omit a transition, or iterate between them. Earlier geospatial frameworks also should not be retrospectively described as GeoAI implementations. GeoAI extends the observational part of this workflow through methods for interpreting spatial information, while geospatial and image-based demolition-waste modelling illustrates the connection between observation and material accounting [I08,U01].

The significance of this decomposition lies in the information exchanged at the boundaries. At S1–S2, observed or inferred characteristics must be associated with a building type, material composition or intensity coefficient. At S2–S3, an inventory becomes an estimate of when and how much material is released. Research on building-size–material-intensity relationships and the importance of lifespan assumptions illustrates why these are substantive modelling operations rather than neutral transfers of numbers [I13,U11]. A downstream estimate may depend on the same uncertain classification or parameter as an upstream estimate. Treating those estimates as independent can therefore change the meaning of the combined assessment.

The next boundary introduces a different question: an estimated outflow is not necessarily an available secondary resource. Material-passport research examines information for end-of-life management, while probabilistic reusability assessment addresses the technical suitability of components [I20,I21]. These approaches locate information needs between release and subsequent use. Recovery conditions, separation, quality and allocation to a reuse or recycling route need to be represented before environmental consequences can be interpreted. Studies comparing environmental assessment methods for component reuse further show the importance of how subsequent use is represented [I22]. S3–S4 and S4–S5 are consequently useful analytical boundaries even where one model calculates several of these activities together.

Uncertainty must be interpreted within this chain of transformations. Michiels and Geeraerd (2020) distinguish incomplete knowledge from variability when examining how these influences are propagated and visualized in life cycle assessment [I25]. For this review, the distinction matters because variation among buildings, uncertainty about an inferred building attribute and alternative future recovery scenarios do not have interchangeable meanings. A probability distribution, an interval, a set of scenarios and a qualitative data-quality description also communicate different kinds of information. An assessment may quantify uncertainty carefully within one model without preserving that representation when its output becomes another model's input.

We use uncertainty continuity to describe the traceable retention or explicit revision of uncertainty information, relevant dependencies and conditions of validity across such transformations. This is an analytical construct of the present review, not a claim that existing studies use a common term or that uncertainty must remain numerically unchanged. Propagation may alter an output distribution; new observations may support updating; aggregation may change the spatial support; and a recovery decision may convert a continuous quantity into an eligibility category. Conversely, an interval may be replaced by a point estimate, or the provenance needed to interpret it may not be reported. The relevant distinction is whether this change is documented and usable downstream. Missing reporting alone does not demonstrate that information was lost in the actual implementation.

Digital twins provide a related body of research on connections between physical entities, digital representations and updating processes. Jones et al. (2020) characterize the concept through a systematic literature review, while Boje et al. (2020) develop directions for semantic construction twins [I27,I26]. Construction-focused reviews examine applications and enabling technologies [I28,I29]. Together, these studies make it useful to examine the functions associated with a digital twin, rather than infer capabilities from its name. A geometric model may represent an asset without maintaining a data connection; an updated representation may support monitoring without implementing probabilistic propagation; and a conceptual architecture may describe services that have not been demonstrated operationally.

Definition differences are particularly relevant to the built environment. Abdelrahman et al. (2025) compare definitions and components across domains and distinguish high-performance real-time twins from long-term decision-support twins [I31]. Their analysis cautions against treating real-time operation, bidirectional exchange and predictive functionality as equally mature or universally required across applications. For material-flow assessment, the appropriate update may occur after a survey, renovation or change in recovery conditions. The methodological question is whether the timing and content of that update preserve the validity of the connected assessment, not simply whether a continuous sensor stream exists.

Research on uncertainty-aware twins offers mechanisms for investigating this question. Kapteyn et al. (2021) formulate coupled asset–twin dynamics using a probabilistic graphical model and demonstrate calibration and updating in an aircraft-related application [I33]. Lin et al. (2021) review uncertainty quantification and software risks in digital-twin-based management and control [I32]. These studies support examination of probabilistic state representation, dependencies and updating as architectural capabilities. They do not establish that the same mechanisms already provide uncertainty continuity from building observation to circular material decisions. Their relevance must be assessed against the entities, quantities, temporal processes and validation conditions of the target workflow.

The three bodies of literature therefore meet at the interfaces between activities. Material-flow studies identify the quantities and transformations that require continuity; uncertainty research explains what must be retained or revised; and digital-twin research offers ways to connect representations, observations and analytical services. Spatial scale, temporal validity and stakeholder information requirements condition these connections. The existence of a scalable software structure, for example, does not itself demonstrate that the underlying material assumptions remain valid across buildings, districts and regions. Similarly, a dashboard can communicate an uncertainty estimate without establishing how it was obtained or propagated.

Existing reviews provide complementary foundations: Pei et al. (2024) examine stock–LCA integration, Michiels and Geeraerd (2020) examine uncertainty and variability, and Abdelrahman et al. (2025) examine DT definitions and components [I35,I25,I31]. The present review brings these perspectives into a common interface-based comparison. It asks which uncertainty-related connections are directly documented, which can be synthesized from compatible studies, and which remain proposals responding to identified limitations. The intended contribution is an evidence-traceable architectural synthesis. It does not presume that all transitions are missing from prior work, nor does it present a newly implemented or validated end-to-end digital twin.
